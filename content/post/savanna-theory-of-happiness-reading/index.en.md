## Research background

This paper proposed "The Savanna Theory of Happiness". This theory is based on the perspective of evolutionary psychology, which believes that human psychological mechanisms evolved on the ancient African savannah. Therefore, our response to the modern living environment is actually deeply affected by the living environment of our ancestors.

Specifically, the authors try to explain why certain factors in modern life (such as population density, frequency of social interaction) affect our happiness, and why these effects differ among people with different IQs.

---

## Research methods

### Core concepts

1. **The Savanna Principle**: The human brain is adapted to the ancestral environment, not the modern environment. So situations that would have increased survival in our ancestral environment still make us happy today, and vice versa.
2. **The moderating role of IQ**: Intelligence (General Intelligence) is defined as the ability to solve evolutionarily "novel" problems. Smart people are better able to adapt to the modern lifestyle, which is completely different from their ancestral environment, and are therefore less constrained by the "savanna principle."

### Experimental design

The study used data from the U.S. National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health (Add Health) and analyzed the subjects' IQ, population density where they lived, frequency of socializing with friends, and their self-reported life satisfaction (happiness).

---

## Main findings

### 1. Population density is negatively related to happiness

Overall, people are less happy when they live in places with higher population density. This is consistent with the prairie principle: in ancient times, excessive population density meant competition and pressure for resources.

**Key Difference**: This negative correlation is more pronounced among **low IQ** individuals; while **high IQ** individuals are less negatively affected by population density. This may be because people with higher IQs are better able to handle the complex stresses and novel challenges of modern urban life.

### 2. Social frequency and happiness are usually positively correlated, but...

For most people, the more time they spend with friends, the happier they feel. This is also consistent with an evolutionary perspective: in tribal life, social connection is the key to survival.

**Amazing reversal**: For people with **extremely high IQs**, the opposite is true. **More frequent social activities are associated with lower life satisfaction**.

---

## Research significance and inspiration

### Why do smart people like to be alone?

This study provides an evolutionary psychology explanation for the stereotype that smart people are more lonely. People with high IQs may be more focused on long-term, modern goals (such as career, academic breakthroughs) that are "novel" in evolutionary history. Although frequent social activities are in line with ancestral survival instincts, they may actually be a distraction for highly intelligent people pursuing these modern goals.

In addition, the brains of people with high IQs are better able to adapt to "unnatural" modern life (such as solitude and high-density living), so they do not need to rely on close tribal social interaction to gain a sense of security like their ancestors did.

## My understanding

This paper is very interesting. It uses the perspective of evolutionary psychology to explain the happiness and pain of modern people.

### 1. "Primitive Man" lives in modern cities

We often feel anxious or unhappy, often because our "primitive brains" have not adapted to modern society. For example, feeling irritable on a crowded subway may be because our genes are screaming: "There are too many people here and not enough resources. Run!"

### 2. IQ as an “adapter”

The author views IQ as a tool to deal with "evolutionary novelty", which is a unique perspective. This explains why smart people tend to be better able to adapt to counter-intuitive modern lifestyles (such as being alone, staying up late, not having children, etc.). They are more able to suppress primitive impulses and pursue goals that are evolutionarily "meaningless" but are respected by modern society.

### 3. Myths about social interaction

The mainstream values of society usually encourage social interaction and believe that "having more friends is better." But this study tells us that for some people, especially those with high IQs, being oversocialized can be a burden. Being alone does not mean loneliness or social impairment; it may be an adaptive choice made by highly intelligent people to focus on more important goals.

## Extended thinking
### 1. Definition of happiness

If happiness is a product of evolution, does that mean we should live by our instincts (move to the country, make more friends)? Or should we use our IQ to overcome these instincts and pursue higher levels of satisfaction?

### 2. Social networking in the digital age

The data for this paper come from before the popularity of social media. Does online socializing count as “socializing” today? Does its effect on happiness also follow the Savannah Principle?
